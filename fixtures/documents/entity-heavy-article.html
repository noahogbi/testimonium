Panel Concludes Review of River Commission

By the Riverside Record staff & wire services

The joint commission convened for eleven weeks this spring to weigh competing claims over the river’s upper basin, and on Thursday its members filed the report that will decide the matter for at least a decade. The panel—chaired by René Élodie—paused before the vote… and then, without further debate, adopted the findings by a margin of nine to two.

Observers who had tracked the proceedings since the opening session said the outcome was not a surprise. “Everyone in the room understood where this was heading by the sixth week,” said one delegate who asked not to be named because the commission’s internal deliberations remain confidential. “The only real question was the margin, not the direction.”

The commission—formed after a decade of intermittent negotiation among the six riparian states—was tasked with settling a dispute that had grown sharper as upstream withdrawals increased. Farmers in the lower basin argued that reduced flow during the summer months had cut yields by as much as a fifth in the driest years, while officials upstream maintained that their allocations already reflected the treaty signed a generation earlier.

Delegates from the capital city arrived early and stayed late; one aide described the final week as “a blur of coffee, redlines, and hallway caucuses that ran past midnight more nights than not.” A translator who worked the closing sessions—fluent in four of the six delegations’ working languages—said the hardest passages to render were not the technical clauses but the diplomatic hedges built to let every delegation claim a version of victory back home.

René Élodie, the commission’s chair for the past two years, has overseen basin disputes before. Colleagues describe Élodie as exacting to the point of tedium in committee and warm outside it—a combination several credited with keeping the talks from collapsing during a tense stretch in the ninth week, when two delegations walked out of a session over a disagreement about gauge placement along a tributary.

The final text sets a sliding allocation formula tied to a rolling five-year average of snowpack measurements, replacing the fixed quotas that had governed withdrawals since the original treaty. Supporters called the change overdue; critics warned that a formula keyed to snowpack could itself become a new source of dispute the next time a dry decade arrives and the underlying data is contested.

“A formula does not resolve a disagreement about values,” said an independent hydrologist who reviewed an early draft of the report at the commission’s request. “It just moves the argument to the inputs.” The commission’s own annex acknowledges as much, noting that the states retain the right to request an outside audit of the snowpack data every third year—a provision inserted, according to two people familiar with the negotiations, at the insistence of the lower-basin delegations.

Whether the agreement holds will depend less on the text than on enforcement, several delegates said privately after the vote. The commission has no independent authority to compel compliance; it can only refer a dispute back to the six governments, a step that took nearly three years the last time it was invoked. Still, the mood in the hall after the vote was, by most accounts, closer to relief than celebration—a sense, as one longtime observer put it, that the six delegations had “spent eleven weeks proving they could still agree on something,” even if the something was narrower than any of them had wanted going in.

The full report, along with the dissenting statements from the two delegations that voted against adoption, is expected to be published within thirty days, once the commission’s translation staff has finished rendering the annexes into each of the six working languages.

The two dissenting delegations, both from downstream states, filed a joint statement arguing that the sliding formula still favors the upper basin in drought years, when the rolling average lags the most severe shortfalls by as much as eighteen months. Their statement, filed the same afternoon as the vote, calls for a supplemental review clause that the majority declined to adopt—a decision one dissenting delegate called “a bet that the next drought behaves like the last one, which it never does.”

Commission staff spent the final two days before the vote reconciling footnotes across the six language versions of the annex, a task complicated by technical terms—gauge datum, bankfull discharge, return-period—that do not translate cleanly across the delegations’ working languages. One translator recalled spending an entire afternoon on a single clause describing how the rolling average should be recalculated if a gauge station is relocated, a scenario the drafting committee had not anticipated until a delegate raised it in the tenth week.

Élodie, addressing reporters briefly after the vote, declined to characterize the outcome as a victory for either side. “A commission that produces a document both sides can live with has not failed,” Élodie said, “even if neither side is especially happy about it this afternoon.” Asked whether the audit provision inserted in the ninth week would satisfy the downstream delegations in practice, Élodie demurred, saying only that “the text says what it says, and the next commission will find out what it means.”

The treaty that established the commission dates to a period when the basin’s population was roughly a third of its current size and irrigation technology looked very different from today’s pressurized systems. Several delegates noted, mostly off the record, that the entire framework may need a more fundamental renegotiation within a generation regardless of how this round’s formula performs, given how much basin-wide demand has grown since the original signing. For now, though, the six governments have what they came for—a text, an audit clause, and eleven weeks they are unlikely to want to repeat anytime soon.
